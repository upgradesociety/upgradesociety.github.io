# The Ğ1 Society & Regional Depopulation (A Strategy For An Abundant Life, Today)

How G1 Could Help Create One of the Most Abundant Societies in Human History

1. Introduction — Why the Current System Feels Broken

In many countries, people feel similar pressure in everyday life.

Even though technology improves and productivity increases, basic stability feels harder to maintain.

Work takes most of our time. Housing costs rise faster than income.

Many jobs feel insecure or disconnected from long-term stability.

These problems are widespread. They are not isolated cases.

Several pressures appear together:

- Financial stress, even during technological progress

- Housing crises across many regions

- Work instability, often without clear long-term security

- Urban burnout driven by cost and density

Here is the paradox:

Human productivity has increased dramatically. But individual life has not improved at the same speed.

This happens because producing more does not automatically give people easier access to housing, time, or security. Access depends on steady income inside systems that price essentials aggressively.

At the same time, people feel constrained even though there is abundance.

Goods and services exist in large quantities. But participating in daily life requires constant money flow. If income stops or costs rise, scarcity appears immediately.

The system does not create stress because things are missing.
It creates stress because access to things is fragile.

2. Rural Depopulation Is The Most Important Tool For A Better World (In My View)

People see rural depopulation as a bad thing when in fact it is one of the most powerful tools we have

This model starts from population trends, not expectations.

Across Europe and the United States, many rural regions have been losing residents for decades. Young people leave. Birth rates fall. Local economies shrink.

This trend is consistent and measurable.

Depopulation creates stable opportunities for repopulation, everywhere

When population declines, demand falls faster than physical supply. Houses remain. Infrastructure remains. But fewer people use them.

Supply stays fixed while demand contracts.

Prices fall. Assets remain unused.

Europe: There&#8217;s thousands of regions that have thousands of cheap houses

In many rural European regions, long-term population decline has created a large housing surplus.

Thousands of houses are listed under €30,000 across multiple countries. These are not rare exceptions. They are ongoing market conditions.

Demand stays low because population continues to decline.

Common features often include:

- Electricity connections

- Water access

- Good road infrastructure

- Sometimes fiber or reliable mobile coverage

Entire villages may contain empty schools, abandoned homes, unused farmland, and very few young residents.

United States: Land Without People (Cheap Plots, Cheap Starts)

In the United States, the same pattern often appears as cheap land instead of extremely cheap houses.

In several rural states and counties, land prices are very low. Houses under $100,000 are still common.

People already move to these areas for:

- Space and low entry costs

- Off-grid or low-overhead living

- Quiet rebuilding outside major cities

Why Depopulation Is an Advantage (Not a Problem)

Depopulation is usually treated as a failure.

In this framework, depopulation lowers entry barriers.

Low prices reduce speculation.
Rent pressure is weak.
Existing power structures are less locked in place.

This creates space for new social and economic patterns to form with less resistance.

This matters because coordination is easier in low-friction environments.

When costs are low and old structures are weak, new habits, norms, and institutions can grow without constantly fighting existing pressures.

3. The Hidden Constraint: Centralized Money

June changes this

Modern money systems are centralized.

Money is created by institutions that are far from everyday economic life. It enters the economy through specific channels, not evenly across society.

This structure shapes how people work, save, and compete.

Several characteristics define this system:

- Money is created far from where it is used

- Distribution favors financial centers

- Access depends on jobs, credit, and rent relationships

The result is predictable.

Money enters the economy unevenly.

This creates constant competition for access.

People must secure income not only to improve their lives, but to maintain basic access to housing, healthcare, and services.

This creates a deeper constraint.

Economic security becomes job-dependent.

Even if production becomes more efficient, people remain tied to employment and rent.

Scarcity today is not caused by technical limits.
It is caused by how money is issued, distributed, and controlled.

4. A Different Premise: What If Money Was Symmetrical?

This alternative starts by changing how money enters the system.

G1 (June) is based on the idea that money can be co-created by participants instead of issued by privileged institutions.

Every member receives money through the same mechanism.
No debt.
No hierarchy.

Key properties include:

- Money co-created by participants

- No privileged issuers

- No debt-based entry

- A living network of users, not a static currency

This changes coordination.

Money becomes a shared baseline, not a competitive prize.

Because everyone receives the same monetary input over time, participation does not depend on winning access through employment or credit.

In this structure, money becomes a coordination layer.

It is not designed to replace all other currencies.
It is not a universal solution.

Instead, it supports local exchange and trust-building.

It provides a neutral and symmetrical medium that allows interaction before large investment or institutional approval is required.

5. People Will Move to Villages Again

Several long-term trends are coming together.

Remote work reduces the need to live near offices.
Many skills can now be used from anywhere.
Urban living costs continue to rise.
At the same time, rural housing remains unused.

Relocation does not happen instantly.

Migration follows social coordination, not geography.

People do not usually move first and figure things out later.
They build relationships first.
They observe norms.
They test cooperation.

Only then do they move.

The sequence is repeatable:

- Online discovery of ideas and people

- Community interaction and repeated communication

- Trust formation through shared activity

- Physical meetings and visits

- Gradual investment and relocation

Because of this order, networks come before settlement.

People do not move alone.
They move toward groups where coordination already exists.

This reduces uncertainty and risk.

6. How G1 Changes the Economics of Place

Cities concentrate demand, competition, and fixed costs.

Housing, services, and time are fully monetized.
This creates constant pressure to earn and spend.

Depopulated regions are different.

They contain unused housing, unused land, and unused capacity.

The contrast is structural:

- Cities have high fixed costs and intense competition

- Depopulated regions have low prices and idle capacity

G1 changes how activity can begin in these places.

Local exchange can start without upfront capital.

Because participants receive money symmetrically, trade and services can appear before investors or institutions approve projects.

This changes the order of development.

Social integration can happen before large capital accumulation.

People can exchange skills, offer services, and build trust using G1.

At the same time, they can continue using fiat systems where necessary.

7. Stories of Transition — Real Lives in Motion

Transitions into new social and economic systems do not start with moving.

They start with communication.

People first discover ideas and communities online.
Online interaction is low-risk and reversible.

There is a clear pattern.

Movement begins online before it becomes physical.

People enter shared spaces.
They observe behavior.
They learn how systems work in practice.

Early participation often happens through:

- Discord servers

- Telegram groups

- Reddit discussions

- Regional G1 community channels

The physical move comes later.

Trust comes before investment.

This reduces uncertainty.

Ethan, the Software Engineer — From San Francisco to Appalachia (USA)

Ethan is 34.
He works remotely as a backend engineer.

He earns a high income.
But he faces high costs and weak community ties.

His transition happens in stages:

- Online entry and observation

- Regional exploration through networks

- Physical visits to markets and meetups

- Gradual financial and geographic transition

Over time, his money usage shifts.

He moves from mostly fiat use toward more G1 participation.

Thomas, the Carpenter — From Paris to Rural Burgundy (France)

Thomas is 41.
He works in construction.

High rent and stress make long-term planning difficult.

His transition follows the same pattern:

- Online discovery and regional groups

- Small jobs through classifieds

- In-person markets and events

- Gradual relocation and monetary transition

In both cases, integration happens before relocation.

8. The Common Pattern — The Migration Protocol (Not the Romantic Story)

These examples follow the same structure.

The process reduces risk by spreading decisions over time.

The protocol looks like this:

- Online discovery

- Community immersion

- Regional networking

- Small economic participation

- Physical visits

- Learning through investment

- Gradual relocation

- Monetary transition

The core principle is simple.

Social alignment comes before geographic change.

People move toward networks.
Not just toward cheap places.

9. The 10-Year Effect — From Individuals to Regional Renaissance

Early changes look small.

Over time, they compound.

The mechanism is cumulative.

Networks grow through repetition, not promotion.

Trust-based invitations replace advertising.

Reinforcing effects include:

- Friends inviting friends

- Markets growing into festivals

- Repeated events signaling stability

Phase 1 — Social Scaling

Events become entry points.

They help new people integrate.

They build trust.

Phase 2 — Capital + Skills Recombine

Unused infrastructure meets new skills.

Tools, knowledge, and space connect.

Idle capacity becomes productive again.

Phase 3 — Crowdfunding Society

Services appear through shared contribution.

Projects are funded by communities.

Not by banks.

Phase 4 — Political Self-Organization

Communities begin to coordinate.

They protect affordability.

They protect safety.

They define shared norms.

Phase 5 — The Stable Community Model

After roughly a decade, regions change.

They become affordable.

They become resilient.

They become predictable.

10. Stable Growth — Not Expansion, But Maturation

This model avoids fast expansion.

Growth is limited by social capacity, not market demand.

Growth remains:

- Slow

- Reputation-based

- Distributed

This prevents boom–bust cycles.

It favors continuity.

Communities focus on:

- Replacement-level population

- Cooperation as normal behavior

- Economic complexity instead of pure volume

- Structural energy efficiency

- Peaceful, high-trust social life
