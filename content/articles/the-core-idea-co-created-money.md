Most people assume money is created by governments or banks.

But historically, many economists and monetary reformers argued something very different:

**Money can be created by people themselves.**

This idea is often called **mutual credit**, **community currency**, or what we could call **co-created money**.

Instead of money being issued by a central authority, it is generated by the participants of a network.

# The Core Idea

Traditional systems create money through:

- banks issuing loans
    
- governments issuing currency
    
- mining (in the case of Bitcoin)
    

But in a co-created money system:

**money is issued collectively by the participants.**

Examples include:

- mutual credit networks
    
- local currencies
    
- universal dividend currencies
    
- proof-of-personhood systems
    

In many models, each participant receives a **periodic issuance simply for being part of the system**.

# Historical Roots

The idea goes back surprisingly far. Here's some of the people that supported this:

**Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (1840s)**  
Proposed a “People’s Bank” where members would create money through mutual credit.

**Alfred Mitchell-Innes (1913)**  
Argued that money is fundamentally **a system of credit between people**, not a commodity.

**Silvio Gesell (1916)**  
Suggested monetary systems designed to serve communities rather than accumulate power.

**Bernard Lietaer (2000s)**  
Studied complementary currencies and how communities can issue their own money.

**David Graeber (2011)**  
Showed historically that money often began as **social credit systems rather than coins**.

# Modern Experiments

Today, new technology is making these ideas possible at scale.

Some projects exploring this concept include:

- **Ğ1 / Duniter** — universal dividend currency
    
- **Circles UBI** — trust-based personal currencies
    
- **mutual credit networks**
    
- **proof-of-personhood systems**
    

These systems try to answer a key question:

> **What if money creation belonged to people rather than institutions?**

# Ancient Credit Systems (David Graeber)

Anthropologist **David Graeber**, in his book _Debt: The First 5000 Years_, explains that **for most of human history money was not coins or banknotes, but systems of credit between people**.

In many traditional societies, economic exchange worked through **social accounting**.

For example:

A baker might give bread to a farmer today.  
Later, the farmer might give vegetables, repair a tool, or help during harvest.

No physical money needed to exist.

Instead, the community maintained a **shared understanding of obligations** — essentially a **distributed ledger of who owed what**.

These systems worked because:

- people knew each other
    
- reputation mattered
    
- exchanges happened repeatedly over time
    
- obligations were remembered socially
    

In practice, this means **money was created through relationships between people**.

When someone provided a good or service, a **credit was created**, and when it was repaid, the credit disappeared.

In other words:

**money emerged from the network of exchanges itself.**

This is fundamentally what **co-created money** means:

- money is not issued by a central authority
    
- it emerges from **mutual credit between participants**
    

# Why These Systems Were Not Inflationary

A key characteristic of these credit systems is that **money was not permanently created**.

Credits appeared **only when someone provided value** and they disappeared **when the obligation was fulfilled**.

So the system behaved differently from modern fiat systems:

- credit was **temporary**
    
- debts were **expected to be settled**
    
- the total amount of money was naturally **limited by real economic activity**
    

In practice:

- if someone produced goods → credit appeared
    
- when that person received goods back → the credit vanished
    

This means money was **continuously created and destroyed through exchange**.

Because of this mechanism, the system tended to remain **naturally scarce and balanced**:

- no authority could print unlimited money
    
- money supply grew only when real exchanges happened
    
- debts acted as a natural constraint
    

From this perspective, these ancient credit economies functioned as **self-regulating monetary systems created by the community itself**.

This is why Graeber’s research suggests that **co-created money is not a new invention** — it reflects one of the **oldest economic mechanisms humans have used to organize exchange**.

# Medieval Village Federations and Free Cities

Long before modern banking systems, many European communities operated with **locally organized economic systems** built on trust, guilds, and mutual obligations.

During the **Middle Ages**, especially between the 11th and 15th centuries, towns and villages across Europe formed **federations and free cities** that governed themselves economically and politically.

Examples include:

- **Hanseatic League** (Northern Europe)
    
- **Italian city-states** such as Florence and Venice
    
- **Free Imperial Cities** of the Holy Roman Empire
    
- **Swiss village confederations** that later formed Switzerland
    

These communities developed **local systems of credit, ledgers, and merchant obligations** that allowed trade to flourish without relying entirely on royal coinage.

Merchants, guilds, and towns often issued **bills of exchange, credit ledgers, or local accounting systems** that functioned as money within the network.

Key characteristics of these systems:

- trade based heavily on **credit between participants**
    
- strong **guild and community governance**
    
- federations of cities cooperating economically
    
- money functioning largely as **a social ledger of obligations**
    

Historians and thinkers such as **Peter Kropotkin** noted that medieval cities and federations often built complex systems of **mutual aid, cooperation, and decentralized economic organization**.

These networks allowed towns to:

- finance large infrastructure projects
    
- coordinate trade across regions
    
- maintain economic resilience without centralized monetary control
    

In many ways, these systems resembled early forms of **community-based monetary networks**, where trust, reputation, and participation within the community played a central role in economic exchange.

# The Miracle of Wörgl

One of the most famous historical experiments in alternative money took place in **Wörgl, Austria in 1932**, during the Great Depression.

At the time:

- unemployment was extremely high
    
- businesses were collapsing
    
- local governments had almost no money
    

The town of Wörgl introduced a **local currency called "stamp scrip"**, inspired by the ideas of **Silvio Gesell**.

The key innovation was **demurrage**:  
the currency lost a small amount of value every month unless a stamp was added.

This encouraged people to **spend the money quickly instead of hoarding it**.

The results were remarkable:

- unemployment fell dramatically
    
- local infrastructure projects were funded
    
- roads, bridges, and public buildings were constructed
    
- economic activity surged while the rest of the country remained in depression
    

The currency circulated **many times faster than national money**.

However, after the experiment became widely known and other towns wanted to copy it, the **Austrian central bank banned the currency** in 1933.

The Wörgl experiment remains one of the most famous demonstrations of how **alternative monetary systems can stimulate local economies**.

# Argentina’s Crédito Networks

During Argentina’s economic crisis in the late **1990s and early 2000s**, millions of people lost access to cash and traditional banking.

In response, communities created large **mutual credit networks** known as **Redes de Trueque** (barter networks).

Participants exchanged goods and services using a community currency called **créditos**.

Key characteristics:

- money was created **within the network**
    
- participants earned créditos by providing goods or services
    
- marketplaces known as **nodos** allowed people to trade locally
    

At the peak of the movement:

- millions of people participated
    
- thousands of trading clubs existed across Argentina
    
- large parallel economies emerged using créditos
    

People traded:

- food
    
- clothing
    
- repairs
    
- services
    
- homemade products
    

For many families, these networks **became a survival mechanism during the economic collapse**.

Although the system eventually faced challenges (including inflation and governance issues), the Argentine crédito networks remain one of the **largest real-world experiments in community-created money**.

# Modern Examples: Ğ1 and Circles UBI

Two of the most interesting modern experiments in **co-created money** are **Ğ1 (Duniter)** and **Circles UBI**.

These systems attempt to implement digitally what earlier thinkers imagined:  
**money issued directly by people rather than banks or states.**

# Ğ1 (Duniter)

**Ğ1**, launched in 2017, is one of the first operational currencies based on the idea of a **Universal Dividend**.

Its core rule is simple:

> Every verified human co-creates the same amount of money periodically.

In practice:

- each member receives a **daily issuance of new currency**
    
- the amount is identical for all participants
    
- money creation is tied to **verified human identities**
    

The system uses:

- a **Web-of-Trust identity system**
    
- the **Duniter blockchain**
    
- community certification to ensure roughly **1 human = 1 account**
    

This model attempts to solve the **early adopter problem** by distributing new money equally across time.

Instead of money concentrating in early participants, the system constantly **rebalances through universal issuance**.

# Circles UBI

**Circles UBI** explores a different approach to human-created money.

Instead of a single currency shared by everyone, **each person issues their own personal currency**.

Every user continuously mints a small amount of their own tokens over time.

The system works through **trust relationships**:

- users decide whose personal currencies they trust
    
- payments move through chains of trust connections
    
- the network converts tokens across trusted paths
    

Example:

- Alice trusts Bob
    
- Bob trusts Carol
    

Alice may accept Carol’s tokens indirectly through Bob.

This creates a **network of interoperable personal currencies**.

Rather than one universal currency, Circles creates a **web of human currencies connected by trust**.
